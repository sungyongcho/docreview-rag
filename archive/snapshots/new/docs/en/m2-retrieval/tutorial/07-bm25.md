## If you have never seen BM25

Lexical search picks documents based on how often the query's words occur in them. It compares characters rather than meaning, which is why it is strong on tokens such as `Item 7A` or `26,974`.

But "picking" and "ordering" are two different jobs. **Finding** the documents that contain a query word is the index's job, and **deciding** which of them belongs on top is the ranking function's job. BM25 belongs to the second job.

**BM25 is not a search engine. It is one scoring formula.** The BM stands for Best Matching, and 25 means it was the twenty-fifth variant tried in that line of work. It became standard when the Okapi system used it at TREC-3 in 1994, and it has been Elasticsearch's default ranking function since Lucene 6 in 2016.

### The questions a ranking function answers

Scoring one document requires deciding three things.

- **How much is this word worth?** A word such as `the` or `risk` that appears in nearly every document must not weigh the same as a rare word such as `goodwill`.
- **How much extra credit does repetition earn?** Nobody disputes that twice beats once, but ten occurrences are rarely twice as relevant as five.
- **What do we do about length?** A long document is more likely to contain any given word. Without a correction it climbs simply for being long.

BM25 assigns one term to each of those three questions. Understand the three terms separately and the whole formula stops being difficult.

## Why here, and not next to M2.4

Placing a second lexical ranking function right beside the first one looks natural. That judgment comes from **shared subject matter**. Build order follows **dependencies** instead.

## Three things ts_rank_cd cannot see

The `ts_rank_cd` used in M2.4 computes cover density, meaning it looks at how closely the matched words cluster inside one document. It is a genuinely useful signal, and **it is a local signal that can be computed from a single document**.

Three values cannot be known from one document alone.

| Value | Meaning | What its absence causes |
|---|---|---|
| `df` | Number of documents containing the word | `risk`, present everywhere, weighs the same as `goodwill`, present once |
| Saturation | The point where repetition stops adding score | The tenth occurrence is worth as much as the second |
| `dl` | Length of this document | A long document wins on volume rather than relevance |

`df` requires counting the whole corpus, and `dl` only means something compared with the average length of other documents. **Cover density cannot use these three because it measures something else, not because it performs badly.** BM25 uses all three.

## Taking the formula apart

Here is the whole formula. There is no need to understand it yet.

#### No file changes — reading the BM25 formula

```text
score(q, d) = SUM over terms t in q of:

    IDF(t) * ( tf * (k1 + 1) ) / ( tf + k1 * (1 - b + b * dl / avgdl) )

    IDF(t) = ln( 1 + (N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5) )
```

The symbols are these.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `tf` | How often this word occurs in this document (term frequency) |
| `df` | How many documents contain this word (document frequency) |
| `N` | Total number of documents in the corpus |
| `dl` | Length of this document (document length, in words) |
| `avgdl` | Average document length across the corpus |
| `k1` | Constant setting the saturation rate, conventionally 1.2 |
| `b` | Constant setting length-normalization strength, conventionally 0.75 |

When the query holds two words, the value above is computed once per word and the results are added. **A document's score is the sum of its per-query-word scores.** That is all.

### IDF — discounting common words

`IDF` abbreviates inverse document frequency. As the name says, it moves opposite to document frequency.

When `df` approaches `N`, meaning the word appears in nearly every document, the numerator approaches zero and the value inside the logarithm approaches one. Since `ln(1)` is zero, **a word present in every document contributes almost nothing.** A word present in only one document keeps a large value.

**But why a logarithm at all?** There are two reasons, and the second is the real one.

First, without it rarity swallows every other signal. Computing `N / df` directly over this project's 9,172 chunks gives this range.

| Chunks holding the word | `N / df` | Under the logarithm |
|---|---|---|
| 9,172 (all) | 1 | 0 |
| 100 | 91.7 | 4.52 |
| 10 | 917 | 6.82 |
| 1 | 9,172 | 9.12 |

Used raw, a word in one chunk outweighs a word in a hundred chunks by a factor of 100. Under the logarithm that factor drops to 2. **At a hundredfold spread, a single broken token from a parsing error outweighs every real content word in the body combined.** The logarithm preserves the ordering of rarity while stopping it from overpowering everything else.

Second, the logarithm turns multiplication into addition. Read `df / N` as the probability that a randomly drawn chunk contains the word, and the information gained by observing that word is `-log(df / N)`, which is `log(N / df)`. **IDF is not a value with a logarithm bolted on, it is that word's information content.**

This matters because independent events multiply their probabilities while their information adds. Both BM25 and TF-IDF build a score by **adding** per-query-word contributions, and the logarithm is what makes that addition valid. **Drop it and summing `N / df` adds quantities that should have been multiplied, which is not a coherent operation.**

The `0.5` terms are smoothing. They hold the denominator away from zero for a lexeme nobody indexed, and they stop the numerator from reaching exactly zero when `df` equals `N`.

What they do not do is keep the logarithm positive. That is the `1 +`, and whether it belongs there is the one place where the published formula has two answers.

### Two IDF variants, and why the default is the newer one

Robertson's original is the ratio on its own. Lucene adds one before the logarithm.

#### No file changes — comparing the two IDF variants

```text
IDF_robertson(t) = ln(     (N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5) )
IDF_lucene(t)    = ln( 1 + (N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5) )
```

The two agree about rare words and disagree about common ones. That ratio drops below one once `df` passes roughly half the corpus, and the logarithm of a number below one is negative. **Robertson's IDF therefore goes negative for any lexeme held by more than half the documents**, while Lucene's floor sits just above zero.

On the sample corpus that is not a subtlety. It is the entire ranking.

| Term | `df` | `IDF_lucene` | `IDF_robertson` |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `market` | 3 | 0.5390 | -0.3365 |
| `risk` | 5 | 0.0870 | -2.3979 |

Both query words are negative under Robertson, so **every match subtracts**, and matching more of the query makes a chunk score worse.

| Document | What it matches | Lucene | Robertson |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| d1 | `market` once, `risk` twice | **0.7793** | -4.1174 |
| d4 | `market` twice, `risk` once | 0.7040 | **-2.3212** |
| d5 | `risk` once, nothing else | 0.1040 | -2.8670 |

Under Lucene `d1` leads and `d5` trails. Under Robertson that inverts, and for exactly the wrong reason: `d5` beats `d1` because it matched **less** of the query.

Negative IDF is not a defect in Robertson's derivation. It falls straight out of reading IDF as log-odds, and on a web-scale corpus the lexemes it punishes really are stop words. It fits this corpus badly because "held by more than half the documents" here describes ordinary vocabulary. `lucene` is the default for that reason, and `robertson` stays selectable because the failure teaches more than the formula does.

### Saturation — repetition earns less and less

Take only the left side of the fraction. Ignoring the length term for a moment leaves `tf * (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1)`.

As `tf` grows this value approaches `k1 + 1` and stops. With `k1` at 1.2 the ceiling is 2.2. At `tf` of 1 it is 1.0, at 2 about 1.375, at 6 about 1.83, and even at 100 it never passes 2.17.

**This is saturation.** There is a meaningful difference between using a word once and twice, but a document using it fifty times is hard to call more than twice as relevant as one using it twenty times. `k1` sets how quickly this curve flattens. A smaller value flattens early; a larger one stays closer to linear for longer.

Classic TF-IDF damps repetition too, but with a logarithm: `1 + log(tf)` in place of the fraction above. Comparing the two at the same term frequencies shows why BM25 replaced it.

| `tf` | `1 + log(tf)` (TF-IDF) | `tf * (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1)` (BM25) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 2 | 1.69 | 1.375 |
| 6 | 2.79 | 1.83 |
| 100 | 5.61 | 2.17 |

**A logarithm grows slowly but never stops. The rational function actually stops, at `k1 + 1`.** That ceiling is what the word saturation names, and a logarithm cannot produce it at any base. Replacing the log on the term-frequency side is one of the changes BM25 made to TF-IDF.

### Length normalization — removing the advantage of being long

Look at the bracket in the denominator, `1 - b + b * dl / avgdl`.

When `dl` equals `avgdl` this value is exactly one and has no effect. A longer-than-average document pushes it above one, enlarging the denominator and losing score. A shorter-than-average document gets the opposite: a bonus.

`b` is the strength of that correction. At zero the bracket is always one and **length normalization is switched off entirely.** At one the length ratio applies in full. 0.75 is the conventional value between them.

### Putting the three pieces together

BM25 then reads like this.

**Discount a word the more common it is, make repetition stop paying at some point, and take back whatever was gained by being long.** Three corrections, multiplied and summed. That is the entire formula.

The three are not variations on one operation. Each reads a different input and has a different mathematical shape, and only the first one is a logarithm.

| Correction | Input | Shape | A logarithm? |
|---|---|---|---|
| `IDF` | `df`, one value per term | `ln(1 + (N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5))` | **yes** |
| Saturation | `tf`, one value per term and chunk | rational, `tf(k1 + 1) / (tf + k1)` | no |
| Length normalization | `dl / avgdl`, one value per chunk | a linear ratio multiplying the denominator | no |

`df` to `IDF` really is **one curve**, and every term in the corpus sits somewhere on it. That intuition is correct, and it applies to exactly one of the three corrections.

That curve is a **function**, not a probability distribution, and a score is computed rather than looked up on it. What gets stored ahead of time is three statistics — `lexeme_stats.df`, `chunk_terms.tf`, and `chunk_lengths.dl` — while `IDF`, saturation, the length term, and the summation all happen at query time.

## Computing it by hand on a sample corpus

Reading a formula and watching numbers move are different experiences. The whole process runs by hand over a five-document corpus.

### Step 1 — lay out the corpus

The fixture lives at `tests/retrieval/fixtures/bm25_sample.json`. Its tokens are stored already stemmed and stripped of stop words. **They are prepared that way because what matters at this step is the ranking function, not text analysis.**

Every token is also its own English snowball stem, which is why `goodwil` is spelled without its second `l`. That is not a typo: it is what `to_tsvector('english', 'goodwill')` produces. Keeping the fixture on stems that survive a round trip means the same five documents can later be loaded into PostgreSQL and produce these exact statistics, so the hand computation and the database are describing one corpus rather than two similar ones.

#### Verify `tests/retrieval/fixtures/bm25_sample.json`

```json
{
  "query": ["market", "risk"],
  "k1": 1.2,
  "b": 0.75,
  "documents": [
    {"id": "d1", "tokens": ["risk", "market", "risk"], "dl": 3},
    {"id": "d2", "tokens": ["risk", "market", "volatil", "interest", "rate"], "dl": 5},
    {"id": "d3", "tokens": ["risk", "risk", "risk", "risk", "risk", "risk"], "dl": 6},
    {"id": "d4", "tokens": ["risk", "market", "market", "goodwil", "impair", "charg", "fiscal", "year"], "dl": 8},
    {"id": "d5", "tokens": ["risk", "revenu", "recogn"], "dl": 3}
  ]
}
```

The five documents are arranged deliberately. `risk` is the common word present in **every** document, while `market` appears in three of the five. `d3` repeats only `risk`, six times. `d4` holds `market` twice but is the longest. `d1` is the shortest and holds `risk` twice.

### Step 2 — extract the statistics

| Document | `tf` (market) | `tf` (risk) | `dl` |
|---|---|---|---|
| d1 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| d2 | 1 | 1 | 5 |
| d3 | 0 | 6 | 6 |
| d4 | 2 | 1 | 8 |
| d5 | 0 | 1 | 3 |

So `N` is 5, and the lengths sum to 25, making `avgdl` 5. For `df`, market is 3 and risk is 5.

### Step 3 — compute IDF

#### No file changes — IDF by hand from the fixture statistics

```text
IDF(risk)   = ln(1 + (5 - 5 + 0.5) / (5 + 0.5)) = ln(1.090909) = 0.0870
IDF(market) = ln(1 + (5 - 3 + 0.5) / (3 + 0.5)) = ln(1.714286) = 0.5390
```

`risk` sits in all five documents, so its value falls to 0.087. `market` is 0.539, **more than six times larger.** Half the conclusion is already fixed at this point: no amount of repeating `risk` can earn much.

### Step 4 — score each document

Put `k1` at 1.2 and `b` at 0.75. For each document, compute the length term in the denominator first, then add the per-word contributions.

#### No file changes — per-document scores by hand

```text
d1  dl=3   1 - 0.75 + 0.75*(3/5) = 0.70    k1*0.70 = 0.84
    market  tf=1  ->  1*2.2/(1+0.84) = 1.1957  *0.5390 = 0.6445
    risk    tf=2  ->  2*2.2/(2+0.84) = 1.5493  *0.0870 = 0.1348
    total = 0.7793

d2  dl=5   1 - 0.75 + 0.75*(5/5) = 1.00    k1*1.00 = 1.20
    market  tf=1  ->  1*2.2/(1+1.20) = 1.0000  *0.5390 = 0.5390
    risk    tf=1  ->  1*2.2/(1+1.20) = 1.0000  *0.0870 = 0.0870
    total = 0.6260

d3  dl=6   1 - 0.75 + 0.75*(6/5) = 1.15    k1*1.15 = 1.38
    market  absent
    risk    tf=6  ->  6*2.2/(6+1.38) = 1.7886  *0.0870 = 0.1556
    total = 0.1556

d4  dl=8   1 - 0.75 + 0.75*(8/5) = 1.45    k1*1.45 = 1.74
    market  tf=2  ->  2*2.2/(2+1.74) = 1.1765  *0.5390 = 0.6341
    risk    tf=1  ->  1*2.2/(1+1.74) = 0.8029  *0.0870 = 0.0699
    total = 0.7040

d5  dl=3   1 - 0.75 + 0.75*(3/5) = 0.70    k1*0.70 = 0.84
    market  absent
    risk    tf=1  ->  1*2.2/(1+0.84) = 1.1957  *0.0870 = 0.1040
    total = 0.1040
```

### Reading the result

| Rank | Document | Score | Why it landed here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | d1 | 0.7793 | Holds `market` and is the shortest |
| 2 | d4 | 0.7040 | Holds `market` twice but was cut for being longest |
| 3 | d2 | 0.6260 | One `market`, and exactly average length |
| 4 | d3 | 0.1556 | Repeats `risk` six times, but that word is nearly worthless |
| 5 | d5 | 0.1040 | Holds only the nearly worthless word |

**Look at `d3`.** It repeats the query word six times, more than any other document, and finishes fourth. Two mechanisms pushed it down together: `risk` sits in every document so its `IDF` is only 0.087, and saturation capped what six repetitions could earn at 1.79. **A ranking function that only counted matches would put `d3` first.** That gap is why this checkpoint exists.

`d1` against `d4` is worth studying too. On word contribution alone `d4` leads, since it holds `market` twice, but at 1.6 times the average length it gave back what it had earned.

### Turning b off flips first place

To see what `b` actually does, switch it off. Set it to zero and recompute with everything else unchanged.

| Document | `b` at 0.75 | `b` at 0 |
|---|---|---|
| d1 | 0.7793 | 0.6586 |
| d2 | 0.6260 | 0.6260 |
| d3 | 0.1556 | 0.1595 |
| d4 | 0.7040 | **0.8281** |
| d5 | 0.1040 | 0.0870 |

**First place flips from `d1` to `d4`.** With length normalization on, `d4` lost to `d1` for being long; the moment it is off, holding `market` twice survives intact and takes first. `d2` is exactly average length, so it scores the same under both settings. **That a document of average length never moves is a good checkpoint for verifying the arithmetic.**

This configuration **must not be called TF-IDF.** Only length normalization was switched off; the saturation term and the probabilistic `IDF` are still there, and they differ from classic TF-IDF's `ln(N / df)`. Name the arm honestly, as in `bm25-no-lennorm`.

#### Verify `tests/retrieval/test_09_bm25.py` — `reference_scores`

<!-- src: tests/retrieval/test_09_bm25.py::reference_scores -->
```python
def reference_scores(
    documents: dict[str, list[str]],
    query: list[str],
    *,
    k1: float = K1,
    b: float = B_PARAM,
    idf: str = "lucene",
) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Score every document with the published BM25 formula, in plain Python.

    This is the oracle the SQL implementation is checked against. It repeats the
    formula rather than importing it so that a mistake in ``bm25_statement`` cannot
    hide by being made twice.

    Repeated query terms are collapsed first. PostgreSQL cannot see them either:
    the statement turns the query into a ``tsvector``, and a ``tsvector`` records
    each lexeme once regardless of how often it was written.
    """
    n_documents = len(documents)
    lengths = {doc_id: len(tokens) for doc_id, tokens in documents.items()}
    avgdl = sum(lengths.values()) / n_documents

    document_frequency: dict[str, int] = {}
    for tokens in documents.values():
        for term in set(tokens):
            document_frequency[term] = document_frequency.get(term, 0) + 1

    scores: dict[str, float] = {}
    for doc_id, tokens in documents.items():
        total = 0.0
        for term in dict.fromkeys(query):
            tf = tokens.count(term)
            if tf == 0:
                continue
            df = document_frequency[term]
            ratio = (n_documents - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5)
            weight = math.log(1 + ratio) if idf == "lucene" else math.log(ratio)
            norm = 1 - b + b * lengths[doc_id] / avgdl
            total += weight * (tf * (k1 + 1)) / (tf + k1 * norm)
        scores[doc_id] = total
    return scores
```

**What to look for in the code**

- A `tf` of zero is skipped. A word absent from the document does not add zero, it **never enters the sum at all.** The result is identical, but in SQL this distinction decides the join.
- Counting `document_frequency` uses `set(tokens)`. `df` is not how many **times** a word occurred but how many **documents** hold it. Confusing these two is the most common mistake in BM25 implementations.
- `avgdl` is computed once outside rather than per document. Putting it inside the loop gives the same answer at a cost that grows with the square of the document count.
- The query runs through `dict.fromkeys` first, so a word written twice is scored once. That is not a stylistic choice: the SQL path turns the query into a `tsvector`, which records each lexeme exactly once no matter how often it was typed. Summing a repeated word twice here would make Python and PostgreSQL disagree on any query that repeats one.
- `idf` selects the variant rather than hard-coding one. The oracle has to be able to reproduce both, or it cannot check the implementation of either.

This function lives in `tests/retrieval/test_09_bm25.py`, not in `app/`. **The oracle is deliberately not importable by the code it checks.** Sharing one helper between the implementation and its test would let a single misreading of the formula satisfy both.

## Moving it to SQL

The Python implementation received its token lists ready-made. On a real corpus the question is where those tokens come from. **The answer is already in the database.** The generated `content_tsv` column M1.4 created holds lexemes that are stemmed and stop-word free, along with their positions.

#### Run — reading the `content_tsv` structure in PostgreSQL

```sql
SELECT lexeme, cardinality(positions) FROM unnest(content_tsv)
```

`unnest` expands a tsvector into rows, and `positions` is the array of places that lexeme occurred. **The length of that position array is exactly `tf`.** Nothing new is parsed, no extension is installed, and no search engine is started.

### 1. The statistics tables

```python
@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class TermStatCounts:
    """Committed row counts for one complete BM25 statistic rebuild."""

    terms: int
    chunks: int
    lexemes: int


async def backfill_term_stats(session: AsyncSession) -> TermStatCounts:
    """Atomically rebuild every BM25 statistic from ``chunks.content_tsv``.

    The function owns one transaction so readers observe either the previous
    complete statistic set or the new one. The source-table lock prevents a
    concurrent chunk write from producing a mixed-corpus snapshot, while the
    derived-table lock serializes competing rebuilds without blocking readers.
    """
    if session.in_transaction():
        raise RuntimeError("backfill_term_stats requires a session without an active transaction")

    async with session.begin():
        await session.execute(text("LOCK TABLE chunks IN SHARE MODE"))
        await session.execute(
            text("LOCK TABLE chunk_terms, chunk_lengths, lexeme_stats IN SHARE ROW EXCLUSIVE MODE")
        )
        await session.execute(delete(ChunkTerm))
        await session.execute(delete(ChunkLength))
        await session.execute(delete(LexemeStat))

        await session.execute(
            text(
                "INSERT INTO chunk_terms (chunk_id, lexeme, tf) "
                "SELECT c.id, t.lexeme, cardinality(t.positions) "
                "FROM chunks AS c CROSS JOIN LATERAL unnest(c.content_tsv) AS t "
                "WHERE t.positions IS NOT NULL AND cardinality(t.positions) > 0"
            )
        )
        await session.execute(
            text(
                "INSERT INTO chunk_lengths (chunk_id, dl) "
                "SELECT chunk_id, SUM(tf) FROM chunk_terms GROUP BY chunk_id"
            )
        )
        await session.execute(
            text(
                "INSERT INTO lexeme_stats (lexeme, df) "
                "SELECT lexeme, COUNT(*) FROM chunk_terms GROUP BY lexeme"
            )
        )

        row = (
            await session.execute(
                select(
                    select(func.count()).select_from(ChunkTerm).scalar_subquery().label("terms"),
                    select(func.count()).select_from(ChunkLength).scalar_subquery().label("chunks"),
                    select(func.count()).select_from(LexemeStat).scalar_subquery().label("lexemes"),
                )
            )
        ).one()
        counts = TermStatCounts(
            terms=int(row.terms),
            chunks=int(row.chunks),
            lexemes=int(row.lexemes),
        )

    return counts
```

**What to look for in the code**

- The order cannot change. `chunk_lengths` and `lexeme_stats` both aggregate `chunk_terms`, so that table must be filled first.
- Deleting before inserting makes the rebuild **idempotent.** Running it twice gives the same result, the same property M1.4's seed provided.
- `COUNT(*)` produces `df`. `chunk_terms` holds one row per chunk-and-lexeme pair, so grouping by lexeme counts the chunks holding it. This is the same reasoning as `set(tokens)` in the Python version.
- `WHERE t.positions IS NOT NULL` blocks a stripped tsvector, which carries lexemes without positions. Such a row would make `tf` null and poison the statistics.
- The whole rebuild is one transaction, and the function refuses a session that is already inside one. A reader either sees the complete previous statistics or the complete new ones. **Half-rebuilt statistics do not raise, they rank wrongly**, and that is far harder to notice.
- `LOCK TABLE chunks IN SHARE MODE` blocks writes to the source while the snapshot is taken. Without it, a chunk inserted midway through can land in `chunk_terms` but not in the `df` aggregate, producing statistics that describe no corpus that ever existed.
- The counts come from three `SELECT count(*)` queries rather than from `rowcount`. The number the caller is told is the number of rows in the committed tables, which is the number that can actually be verified afterwards.
- One limit is worth knowing. PostgreSQL stores at most 256 positions per lexeme, so `tf` stops there. Chunks of 500 to 1200 characters never reach it, but a larger chunking arm in M3.4 should be checked.

#### Modify `app/retrieval/bm25.py` — `_idf_expression`

<!-- src: app/retrieval/bm25.py::_idf_expression -->
```python
def _idf_expression(
    variant: BM25Idf,
    corpus_size: SQLColumnExpression[int],
    document_frequency: SQLColumnExpression[int],
) -> SQLColumnExpression[float]:
    """Return the SQL inverse document frequency for one lexeme.

    Both published variants are available because they disagree about common
    terms. Robertson's original is ``ln((N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5))``, which turns
    negative once a lexeme appears in more than half the corpus; a chunk is then
    penalised for containing it, and on a small corpus that inverts the ranking
    outright. Lucene adds one before the logarithm, so its idf never drops below
    zero and a common term merely stops contributing. Lucene is the default for
    that reason; Robertson stays selectable because the difference is the whole
    lesson.

    ``df`` is clamped to the corpus size because a lexeme cannot occur in more
    chunks than exist. The clamp only bites on stale statistics: deleting chunks
    cascades their ``chunk_terms`` and ``chunk_lengths`` rows away but leaves
    ``lexeme_stats`` untouched, and an unclamped Robertson numerator would then go
    negative and make PostgreSQL raise on ``ln`` of a nonpositive argument.
    """
    if variant not in BM25_IDF_VARIANTS:
        raise ValueError("idf must be 'lucene' or 'robertson'")
    size = cast(corpus_size, Float)
    df = func.least(cast(document_frequency, Float), size)
    ratio = (size - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5)
    if variant == "lucene":
        return func.ln(1.0 + ratio)
    return func.ln(ratio)
```

**What to look for in the code**

- The variant is validated here rather than trusted. An unknown name raises while the statement is being built, not after PostgreSQL has already run something meaningless.
- `func.least(df, N)` is not defensive tidying. `chunk_terms` and `chunk_lengths` cascade away when a chunk is deleted, but **`lexeme_stats` has no foreign key and does not.** Delete chunks without rebuilding and `df` can exceed `N`, which makes Robertson's numerator negative and PostgreSQL raise on `ln` of a nonpositive argument. The clamp turns a crash into a stale-but-finite score, and `--rebuild-bm25-stats` is what actually fixes it.
- Lucene needs no such protection even unclamped: `1 + (N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5)` stays positive for every non-negative `N` and `df`. **The variant that cannot go negative also cannot fail**, which is a second reason it is the default.

### 4. The scoring statement

The query becomes a **bag of words**, scored term by term. M2.4's relaxed disjunction already decides "does this chunk match any of these words"; what it cannot do is weigh them, because PostgreSQL keeps no corpus statistics. BM25 needs the list of words precisely so each one can carry its own `idf`, saturation, and length correction. Running the query through `to_tsvector` makes the stemming rules on both sides identical.

#### Modify `app/retrieval/bm25.py` — `bm25_statement`

<!-- src: app/retrieval/bm25.py::bm25_statement -->
```python
def bm25_statement(
    query: str,
    k: int,
    filters: RetrievalFilters | None = None,
    *,
    k1: float = DEFAULT_BM25_K1,
    b: float = DEFAULT_BM25_B,
    idf: BM25Idf = DEFAULT_BM25_IDF,
) -> Select[Any]:
    """Build a bound BM25 query with shared filters and deterministic ordering."""
    normalized_k1, normalized_b = _validated_parameters(query, k, k1, b)
    active_filters = filters or RetrievalFilters()

    query_terms = select(
        func.unnest(
            func.tsvector_to_array(
                func.to_tsvector(
                    TEXT_SEARCH_CONFIG,
                    bindparam("bm25_query", value=query, type_=String()),
                )
            )
        ).label("lexeme")
    ).subquery("bm25_query_terms")
    corpus = select(
        func.count(ChunkLength.chunk_id).label("n"),
        func.avg(ChunkLength.dl).label("avgdl"),
    ).subquery("bm25_corpus")

    k1_param = bindparam("bm25_k1", value=normalized_k1, type_=Float())
    b_param = bindparam("bm25_b", value=normalized_b, type_=Float())
    document_length = cast(ChunkLength.dl, Float)
    idf_term = _idf_expression(idf, corpus.c.n, LexemeStat.df)
    length_norm = 1.0 - b_param + b_param * document_length / corpus.c.avgdl
    saturation = (ChunkTerm.tf * (k1_param + 1.0)) / (ChunkTerm.tf + k1_param * length_norm)
    score = cast(func.sum(idf_term * saturation), Float).label("score")

    scores = (
        select(ChunkTerm.chunk_id.label("chunk_id"), score)
        .select_from(ChunkTerm)
        .join(query_terms, query_terms.c.lexeme == ChunkTerm.lexeme)
        .join(LexemeStat, LexemeStat.lexeme == ChunkTerm.lexeme)
        .join(ChunkLength, ChunkLength.chunk_id == ChunkTerm.chunk_id)
        .join(corpus, true())
        .group_by(ChunkTerm.chunk_id)
        .subquery("bm25_scores")
    )

    return (
        select(
            Chunk.id.label("chunk_id"),
            Chunk.doc_id,
            Chunk.item,
            Chunk.kind,
            Chunk.citation,
            Chunk.start_char,
            Chunk.end_char,
            Chunk.source_sha256,
            Chunk.body,
            Chunk.context_header,
            Chunk.index_text,
            scores.c.score,
        )
        .select_from(Chunk)
        .join(scores, scores.c.chunk_id == Chunk.id)
        .join(Document, Document.doc_id == Chunk.doc_id)
        .where(*_filter_predicates(active_filters))
        .order_by(
            scores.c.score.desc(),
            Chunk.doc_id.asc(),
            Chunk.source_sha256.asc(),
            Chunk.start_char.asc(),
            Chunk.end_char.asc(),
            Chunk.id.asc(),
        )
        .limit(bindparam("bm25_k", value=k, type_=Integer()))
    )
```

**What to look for in the code**

- It maps line by line onto the Python reference. `idf_term` is the `IDF` computation, `saturation` is the whole fraction, and `func.sum` adds the per-query-word contributions. **It is the same formula written in two languages.**
- `bindparam("bm25_query")` keeps user input a value. The injection contract M2.4 established does not loosen because the ranking function changed.
- `k1`, `b`, and `idf` are parameters, not literals embedded in the expression. Leave them as constants and there is no way to run a length-normalization-off arm, or a second IDF variant, in M3.4.
- `to_tsvector` is applied to the query, then `tsvector_to_array` turns it into lexemes. A `tsvector` holds each lexeme once, which is where the deduplication the Python oracle imitates actually happens.
- `corpus` is computed inside the statement rather than cached in Python. A cached `N` that drifts from the table does not raise; it produces **quietly wrong scores.**
- The filter predicates are the ones M2.3 and M2.4 share. A third retrieval path filtering differently breaks the guarantee that every component sees the same document set.
- Filters narrow the candidates and nothing else. `N`, `avgdl`, and `df` are still read from the whole corpus, so restricting a search to one filing does not silently redefine what a rare word is.

```python
async def bm25_search(
    session: AsyncSession,
    query: str,
    k: int = 5,
    filters: RetrievalFilters | None = None,
    *,
    k1: float = DEFAULT_BM25_K1,
    b: float = DEFAULT_BM25_B,
    idf: BM25Idf = DEFAULT_BM25_IDF,
) -> list[ChunkHit]:
    """Rank chunks by BM25 and return complete, deterministically ordered hits."""
    result = await session.execute(bm25_statement(query, k, filters, k1=k1, b=b, idf=idf))
    return [ChunkHit.model_validate(row) for row in result.mappings().all()]
```

**What to look for in the code**

- The first four parameters match `lexical_search` exactly and the return type is `list[ChunkHit]`. **The whole basis for swapping the module without touching fusion or the service is in that one line.**
- 1.2, 0.75, and `lucene` are conventional starting values, not values measured to win on this corpus. They are a starting point for M3.4 and are not presented as tuned.
- Everything past `filters` is keyword-only. `bm25_search(session, query, 5, filters)` reads identically to `lexical_search(session, query, 5, filters)`, and the tuning knobs cannot be passed positionally into the four parameters the adapter boundary fixed.
- Ordering falls through to the same source tie-breakers as every other strategy. Equally scored results must not reorder between runs.
- The score returned is a BM25 score. It compares with neither cosine similarity nor `ts_rank_cd`. **That is exactly why M2.5 fuses ranks rather than scores.**

## Reaching it from configuration and the CLI

A ranking function nobody can select is not a second path, it is dead code. Four settings expose it, and the CLI can override each for one command.

| Setting | Environment variable | Default | What it selects |
|---|---|---|---|
| `lexical_ranker` | `LEXICAL_RANKER` | `ts_rank_cd` | Which lexical path `retrieve` runs |

| `bm25_b` | `BM25_B` | `0.75` | Length-normalization strength |

## Focused tests and the contracts they protect

#### Run `tests/retrieval/test_09_bm25.py`

```bash
uv run pytest tests/retrieval/test_09_bm25.py -q
```

The tests line up three values. **The hand-computed table, the Python reference, and the SQL implementation must produce the same numbers.**

| Value the test breaks | Contract being protected |
|---|---|
| A Python result differing from the fixture's expected scores | The formula matches the published formula. |
| A SQL result differing from Python | The statement transcribed the formula faithfully. |
| An implementation counting `df` as occurrences rather than documents | The definition of document frequency holds. |
| A rebuild run twice producing different rows | Statistics are derived data and idempotent. |
| A query assembled by string concatenation instead of binding | No injection path exists. |
| A filter behaving unlike the other paths | Every component sees the same document set. |
| `k1`, `b`, or `idf` read from a module constant | M3.4 can vary all three. |
| A return type that is not `ChunkHit` | The adapter boundary holds. |
| Lucene's IDF turning negative at any document frequency | The default variant has a floor at zero. |
| Robertson's IDF failing on stale statistics | `df` is clamped to the corpus size, so the logarithm stays defined. |
| A repeated query word scored twice | Python and PostgreSQL agree about what a query contains. |
| `lexical_ranker` failing to change which path runs | Configuration selects the ranker; the code does not. |

The rule for moving on is simple: do not accept M2.9 if the scores were never checked against arithmetic done outside the code, if a caller cannot vary `k1`, `b`, or `idf`, or if anything below the adapter had to be edited to attach the new path.

The live PostgreSQL tests in the same file matter more than their runtime suggests. They load these five fixture documents into real tables, rebuild the statistics from the generated `content_tsv`, and require the resulting scores to match the Python oracle to within a billionth. **That is the assertion that proves the SQL is BM25** rather than an expression that merely runs and returns numbers in a plausible order.

## Proving the swap cost nothing

Now the claim from the start of this chapter can be checked. Run the CLI once per lexical path and compare the component rankings.

#### Run — the same query through each lexical ranker

```bash
uv run python -m app.retrieval --query "NVDA 2024 R&D" --k 5 --lexical-ranker ts_rank_cd
uv run python -m app.retrieval --query "NVDA 2024 R&D" --k 5 --lexical-ranker bm25
```

The `component_rankings.lexical` arrays in the two payloads differ. Everything else about the two runs is identical.

Attaching the second path cost `rrf_fuse` nothing, `hybrid_search` nothing, and `rerank_hits` nothing. `retrieve` gained parameters that select and forward a ranker, and `__main__.py` gained flags that fill them, but neither learned anything about how BM25 scores. **Fusion still consumes ranks, so it never had to be told that a second scoring scale exists.** Had it needed to, the boundary drawn at M2.4 was weaker than it looked, and that is also a finding worth recording.

**Do not pick a default here.** Both arms enter the M3.4 ablation matrix side by side, and the production ranking function written into the docs is chosen by measured recall, MRR, and latency. Picking now because one formula looks more sophisticated betrays the very reason this project has an evaluation layer.

## What you should be able to explain now

- **Why can `ts_rank_cd` not tell a common word from a rare one?**
  - **Answer:** It scores from one document alone and never consults corpus-wide statistics, so document frequency never reaches the ranking.
- **What does `k1` change, and what does `b` change?**
  - **Answer:** `k1` sets how quickly repeated occurrences stop adding score, and `b` sets how much a longer-than-average document is cut.
- **Why did `d3`, which repeats the query word six times, finish fourth?**
  - **Answer:** That word appears in every document so its `IDF` was only 0.087, and saturation capped the six repetitions at a contribution of 1.79.
- **Why, when `b` is set to zero, does first place become `d4`?**
  - **Answer:** `d4` is the longest document and was therefore cut hardest by length normalization; switching it off leaves its two occurrences of `market` intact.
- **Why must a length-normalization-off configuration not be called TF-IDF?**
  - **Answer:** The saturation term and the probabilistic `IDF` are still present, and both differ from the classic formula.
- **Why does counting `df` use `set(tokens)`?**
  - **Answer:** `df` is the number of documents containing a word, not its occurrence count, so several occurrences inside one document must still add only one.
- **Why is it safe to drop the statistics tables?**
  - **Answer:** They derive entirely from `content_tsv`, so a rebuild restores the original values exactly and no evidence lives only there.
- **Why does the default IDF variant add one before taking the logarithm?**
  - **Answer:** Without it the value goes negative for any word in more than half the corpus, so matching a common word subtracts, and on a small corpus a chunk that matches less of the query can outrank one that matches more.
- **Why is `df` clamped to the corpus size when the tables are rebuilt together?**
  - **Answer:** Deleting chunks cascades `chunk_terms` and `chunk_lengths` away but leaves `lexeme_stats` untouched, so `df` can outlive the chunks it counted and exceed `N`.
- **Why is the Python reference implementation kept in the test file rather than in `app/`?**
  - **Answer:** An oracle imported by the code it checks stops being independent, and one misreading of the formula would then satisfy both sides.
- **Why is rebuilding the statistics a separate explicit command instead of part of every query?**
  - **Answer:** They are derived from a particular set of chunks, so they are only wrong when the chunks change, and rebuilding on any other occasion is pure cost.
- **Why does this checkpoint refuse to name a winner?**
  - **Answer:** Comparison requires the recall, MRR, and latency measured in M3.4, and an elaborate formula guarantees no better result.
